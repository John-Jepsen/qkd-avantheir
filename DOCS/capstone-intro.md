# Adaptive Machine Learning Defenses for Quantum Key Distribution: Evidence from a Co-Evolving Attacker and Defender

*John Jepsen*

*MSCS Capstone — Avantheir QKD Integration Initiative*

## Abstract

Quantum Key Distribution, or QKD, is a way to share secret keys that is protected by the laws of physics rather than by the difficulty of math problems. As companies start to use it in real networks, QKD is being plugged into the same systems that already protect our daily traffic, like web sessions, virtual private networks, and the channels that microservices use to talk to each other. This raises a simple but important question: the standard way to detect an eavesdropper is to watch for a fixed error rate on the line, but what happens when an attacker is smart enough to stay just under that line? Earlier work in quantum cryptography has proven that QKD is secure in theory, but very little of that work tests how the detection rules actually behave when an attacker is allowed to learn and adapt. In this project, we replace the fixed error-rate rule with a small group of machine learning models that watch the link together and decide, as a team, whether the channel looks clean or tampered with. We then build an evolutionary "gym" where simulated attackers are pitted against these models, generation after generation, while the attackers are forced to obey the physical limits of a real QKD link. The defender retrains after each round using examples of the latest attacks. The headline result is clear: against the old fixed rule, evolved attackers slipped past detection roughly a third of the time, and that number got worse with every generation. Against our adaptive defender, the attackers never gained ground — their success rate stayed essentially flat for fifty rounds of evolution, and the defender still kept its accuracy on normal traffic above ninety percent. Two simpler lessons came out of the same experiments. First, the type of model matters more than the size. We compared two common machine learning classifiers, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting. Both are built out of decision trees, but they combine those trees in different ways. Random Forest trains many trees independently and averages their votes, which makes its decisions smooth and hard to nudge across the line. Gradient Boosting trains trees one after another, each one trying to fix the last one's mistakes, which makes its decisions sharper but more brittle. Gradient Boosting actually scored higher on a one-time accuracy test, but in the gym attackers learned to fool it more and more easily over time, while Random Forest stayed steady. Second, retraining too aggressively on attack samples actually hurt the defender on normal traffic, so a lighter touch worked better. To make sure these results are useful in practice, we mapped the adaptive defender onto the kinds of hybrid quantum-and-classical setups that today's QKD vendors actually sell, and we confirmed that nothing in our design breaks the standard interfaces those vendors already use. The broader takeaway is that QKD security in the field is not just a question of whether the protocol is provably secure, but whether the software around it can keep up with attackers that learn. We argue that future evaluations of QKD systems should include this kind of co-evolving test, not just a one-time check against a fixed threshold.

**Keywords:** Quantum Key Distribution; eavesdropping detection; adaptive defense; machine learning security; co-evolving attacker and defender; hybrid quantum-classical encryption.
